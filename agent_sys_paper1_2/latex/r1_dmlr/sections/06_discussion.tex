\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The benchmark and its measurement protocol are designed to make failure
dynamics—onset, propagation, and the closing recovery window—empirically
observable, and to test whether a calibrated early-warning baseline can
transfer to held-out task, fault, and composition regimes. This section
discusses the interpretational boundaries that apply regardless of the
observed outcomes, and the specific comparisons the confirmatory analysis
will address.

\subsection{Interpretation of primary results}

The confirmatory analysis is structured around two preregistered claims.
The first (H1) is that execution-grounded state-difference and evidence
histories predict terminal scientific failure earlier and more reliably
than terminal checks, self-confidence, or trace-only critics on held-out
task and fault families. The second (H2) is that warning lead time and
causal fault class jointly predict whether a trajectory remains
recoverable after controlling for task difficulty, model, and fault
severity. Both claims are evaluated against their preregistered evidence
thresholds in Section~\ref{sec:results}. If the warning lead-time
improvement and the recoverability decline both survive their sensitivity
conditions, the effect sizes are interpreted in terms of actionable steps
and recovery opportunity. If only one passes, the surviving claim is
interpreted without implying the other. If neither passes, the benchmark's
value as a reusable measurement artifact is discussed independently of the
predictive claims, and the predeclared fallback to a benchmark-and-negative
measurement paper is followed.

\subsection{Causal versus predictive language}

Results from controlled fault injection (randomized assignment with known
causal parent) support causal interpretation under the standard assumptions
of no unmeasured confounders within the randomized block and no interference
across trajectories. Results from naturally occurring L4 failures are
reported with associational language only; their fault labels are
adjudicated but are not causally known. Warning lead time is a predictive
claim: the warning model observes correlates of failure, not necessarily
causes. Any sentence in the manuscript that uses causal language for an
injected fault and predictive language for a warning result preserves this
distinction.

\subsection{Coverage limits}

All primary results are conditional on the sampled models, task families,
fault mechanisms, and resource budgets. The benchmark tests two model
configurations in the primary study, four workload families, and six
retained fault mechanisms after pilot filtering. Generalization to
substantially different agent architectures, scientific domains, or fault
types is not claimed without further evidence. The external-validity L4
arm is sized after the pilot and is explicitly a transfer test, not a
population estimate. Repeated trajectories within a task are clustered
observations; they do not inflate the independent sample count, and all
reported intervals respect task-template and task-instance nesting.

\subsection{Falsification failures and negative findings}

All predeclared sensitivity tests that fail are reported. This includes any
failure law that does not replicate across two workload families, any
warning improvement that disappears under terminal-event removal, any
attribution degradation on unseen families, and any recoverability result
explained entirely by severity rather than delay. A negative finding on a
preregistered test is retained in the artifact and discussed; it does not
trigger post-hoc outcome substitution. The protocol deviations log records
any pilot-to-confirmatory instrument change with its scientific rationale,
and the confirmatory sample size is justified by the pilot-driven power
simulation rather than by a post-hoc appeal to the observed effects.

\subsection{Comparison with prior benchmarks}

The failure-dynamics measurements are positioned against the
failure-taxonomy results in MAST-Data~\citep{mast2025}, the critical-step
localization in AgentRx~\citep{agentrx2026}, and the intervention-oriented
taxonomy in Aegis~\citep{aegis2025}. The comparison is made on the temporal
and causal dimensions that those resources do not control: governed
injection with known causal parents, structured state-difference
observation, and frozen transfer splits. Whether the execution-grounded
approach reveals structure that static or post-hoc labeling does not is an
empirical question answered by the confirmatory analysis, not assumed by
the framing.
